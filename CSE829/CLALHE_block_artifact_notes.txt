1. WHAT YOU WERE SEEING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The output had straight lines cutting the picture into rectangles. They were
most obvious in the smooth, out-of-focus background (top-left crowd, the blurred
region behind the shoulder) and invisible in busy areas such as the face and the
shirt texture.

For crybaby.jpeg the pipeline produced these parameters:

    peaks found        = 55
    valleys found      = 54
    CBD (Eq. 2)        = 5
    I_CL (Eq. 3)       = 1.000
    ONS (Eq. 5)        = 6
    SID (Eq. 6)        = sub_h = 400, sub_w = 341
    resulting grid     = 2 rows x 3 columns

So the seams were expected at:

    horizontal seam at y = 400
    vertical  seams at x = 341 and x = 682

which is exactly where the lines appeared in your screenshot. The artifact was
not random noise or a JPEG problem - it was the subimage grid itself becoming
visible.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHY THE SUBIMAGE BOUNDARIES WERE VISIBLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The cause was in part2_enhance. The original loop did this:

    subimage     = gray_image[y_start:y_end, x_start:x_end]     # cut
    enhanced_sub = clahe.apply(subimage)                        # equalize alone
    enhanced_image[y_start:y_end, x_start:x_end] = enhanced_sub # paste back

Each subimage was cut out, passed through its OWN separate call to
cv2.createCLAHE(...).apply(), and pasted straight back with a hard edge.

Histogram equalization is a per-pixel intensity remapping: it builds a lookup
table T from the histogram of whatever region it is given, then replaces every
pixel value v with T(v). Six independent subimages means six independent
histograms, therefore six DIFFERENT lookup tables T1..T6.

Now consider two neighbouring pixels sitting on opposite sides of x = 341:

    left  pixel, input value 118  ->  T1(118) = 131
    right pixel, input value 119  ->  T2(119) = 147

The inputs are almost the same (the background is smooth there), but they are
remapped by unrelated functions, so the outputs differ by a visible amount. Do
this down the whole column and you get a continuous, perfectly straight step in
brightness - a seam. The human visual system is extremely good at detecting
straight, extended edges of even 2-3 grey levels, which is why the lines were so
obvious even though I_CL was only 1.0 (very mild enhancement).

Three follow-on points that explain the specific look of the artifact:

  (a) Why the seams show up in the blurred background but not on the face.
      The step size is roughly constant everywhere, but in textured regions it
      is masked by the local detail. In a smooth gradient there is nothing to
      hide it, so it reads as a hard line.

  (b) Why you did NOT see a 5x5 grid of lines inside each subimage.
      CBD = 5, so OpenCV's CLAHE internally divides each subimage into 5x5
      tiles, each with its own LUT. Those would produce exactly the same kind
      of seam - except OpenCV bilinearly interpolates between the four nearest
      tile LUTs for every pixel (Zuiderveld's original algorithm, the paper's
      reference [28]), which makes the transition smooth. That interpolation is
      confined to a single .apply() call. Six separate .apply() calls have
      nothing tying them together, so the boundaries BETWEEN subimages stayed
      sharp.

  (c) Why it appeared in both grayscale and colour.
      For colour input, enhance() converts to LAB and processes only the L
      (lightness) channel, then converts back. The seams are therefore pure
      luminance steps in both cases and look identical. The colour conversion
      is not involved in the artifact.

This is not a CLALHE-specific problem. It is the classic block artifact of
plain, non-interpolated Adaptive Histogram Equalization - historically the
exact defect that motivated the interpolation step in CLAHE. The paper itself
describes this defect in Section III, calling it the 'halo effect' and
describing it as "a distinct grid-like pattern surrounding edges" - but it
applies that reasoning only to AHE's tiles, never to its own subimage grid.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. DOES THE PAPER MENTION BILINEAR INTERPOLATION? - NO. VERIFIED.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This was your direct question, so it gets its own section. Short answer: you
did not miss a blending step. There is none to miss.

3.1  The phrase "bilinear interpolation" appears exactly ONCE in the entire
     paper, on p. 62606, and it is NOT about CLALHE. It is in the literature
     review, describing ACE-HM (reference [78], Liu et al.), one of the prior
     methods being surveyed:

         "Bilinear interpolation also reduces blocking artifacts, which
          eliminates the problems caused by block-wise histogram processing
          and makes the transitions between image regions smoother."

     So the authors were explicitly aware that block-wise histogram processing
     causes blocking artifacts, and that bilinear interpolation is the standard
     cure - they wrote that sentence about someone else's method. They did not
     carry it into their own.

3.2  What CLALHE actually specifies for the merge. Section V.B lists Part 2's
     five steps; step (v) is only:

         "combination of the enhanced subimages to produce the final enhanced
          image"

     and the body text adds:

         "Once all the subimages have been enhanced, they are then combined to
          construct the resultant image. ... The resulting image is a composite
          of enhanced subimages."

     No overlap, no weighting, no interpolation, no smoothing - "combined" and
     "composite" are the only words used.

3.3  The decisive line is in Section IX (Computational Complexity), item (viii),
     where the authors enumerate what each step costs:

         "(viii) Merging of subimages involves their concatenation, which is
          linear in terms of total pixels and results in a linear complexity
          of O(M.N)."

     CONCATENATION. That is unambiguous - the subimages are stuck together edge
     to edge, exactly as your original code did. A blended or interpolated merge
     would not be described as concatenation, and the complexity budget leaves
     no room for one.

3.4  Conclusion: your original part2_enhance was a CORRECT implementation of the
     paper's Part 2. The seams are a property of the published method, not a
     bug you introduced. The paper claims its output is free of "washed-out or
     unwanted artifact details" (Section V) and highlights "minimization of
     unwanted artifacts" (Section X), but nothing in the described algorithm
     prevents subimage seams.

3.5  Worth noting for your report: POSHE - reference [29], one of the seven
     methods CLALHE is benchmarked against - is literally "Partially Overlapped
     Sub-block Histogram Equalization". The overlap-based cure for this exact
     artifact is sitting in the paper's own comparison table.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. WHAT I CHANGED, AND WHY THIS APPROACH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

There were two ways to remove the seams:

  Option A - drop the manual subdivision entirely and make a single
  cv2.createCLAHE call over the whole image with tileGridSize =
  (cols*CBD, rows*CBD). Same effective tile density, and OpenCV's internal
  interpolation removes every seam for free.

  Option B - keep the paper's subdivision and blend the subimage boundaries.

Option A is less code, but it collapses Part 2 of the paper into a single CLAHE
call, so the "Local" in CLALHE - the independent per-subimage histograms - would
no longer exist. You asked to stay faithful to the paper, so I implemented
Option B.

CHANGES MADE (all inside CLALHE.py):

  1. New helper: _blend_profile(...)
     Builds a 1-D weight curve for one subimage along one axis. The weight is
     1.0 in the interior of the subimage, and falls linearly to 0.0 across a
     band of width 2*margin centred on each SHARED boundary. No ramp is applied
     at the outer image border.

  2. part2_enhance(...) now takes a blend_ratio argument (default 0.25) and its
     write-back loop was restructured:

       - Read window is enlarged. Each subimage is still defined by exactly the
         same core rectangle from Eq. (5)/(6), but it is READ with an extra
         margin (25% of the subimage size) on every side that touches a
         neighbour, clipped at the image border.

       - CLAHE is still applied once per subimage, independently, with the same
         optimal CBD and I_CL. Nothing about the parameter scheme changed.

       - Write-back is now a weighted accumulation instead of a hard paste:

             accumulator  += enhanced_sub * weights
             weight_total += weights
             result = accumulator / weight_total

         where weights = w_y[:,None] * w_x[None,:] (separable 2-D cross-fade).

  3. The result is rounded, clipped to [0,255] and cast back to the input dtype,
     since the accumulation happens in float64.

WHY THIS PARTICULAR WEIGHTING

The two ramps are deliberately centred ON the boundary, not started at it.
Subimage A's weight falls 1 -> 0 over [b-m, b+m]; subimage B's weight rises
0 -> 1 over the same interval. Linear ramps that are mirror images sum to
exactly 1.0 at every position in the overlap, so:

    - the composite is a true convex combination of the two enhanced versions
      (no brightening or darkening of the seam region, no halo band);
    - the output is continuous across the boundary, because at the boundary
      itself both subimages contribute 0.5 and the value slides smoothly from
      pure-A to pure-B;
    - the normalisation by weight_total is mathematically redundant in the
      interior, but it is kept because it makes the border/corner cases
      (clipped read windows, odd remainders in the last row/column) correct
      automatically.

Conceptually this is the same trick CLAHE already performs internally between
its CBD x CBD tiles, applied one level up, between the ONS subimages. It is
also, in effect, what POSHE [29] does with its partially overlapped sub-blocks
and what ACE-HM [78] does with bilinear interpolation - both of which the paper
cites approvingly in its own literature review.

WHAT IS PRESERVED

    - ONS and SID (Eq. 5, Eq. 6)        unchanged
    - number and placement of subimages unchanged
    - one independent CLAHE per subimage unchanged
    - CBD and I_CL from Part 1          unchanged
    - blend_ratio = 0.0 reproduces the original hard-cut behaviour exactly,
      so you can A/B the two for your report.

MEASURED RESULT

Seam strength was measured as the mean absolute intensity step across the
boundary row/column, divided by the median step of nearby rows/columns. A
ratio of 1.0 means "indistinguishable from ordinary local image gradient";
higher means a visible artifact. The unenhanced input is included as the
baseline, because the photo has some real content near y=400.

                          x=341      x=682      y=400
    original (baseline)   1.27x      1.02x      2.29x
    hard cut (before)     1.72x      2.14x      3.92x
    blended  (after)      1.28x      0.95x      2.38x

After blending, every boundary is back to the baseline level of the original
photograph, i.e. the algorithm no longer adds any step of its own. (The 2.29x
at y=400 is a real horizontal edge in the source image, not an artifact - note
that the unenhanced input shows it too.) This was also confirmed visually on a
side-by-side render.

COST

25% overlap on split axes means each subimage is read about 1.5x larger in each
split direction, so roughly 2x more pixels are pushed through CLAHE overall.
For these image sizes it is not noticeable. Lower blend_ratio = cheaper and
sharper transitions but a higher chance of a residual seam; higher = smoother
but the subimages' distinct local adaptation gets averaged away over a wider
band. 0.25 was comfortably sufficient here.

Note this does change the paper's O(M.N) complexity claim by a constant factor
(still linear, roughly 2x the pixel work in Part 2).

5. AUDIT - WHAT YOUR CODE GOT RIGHT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Checked against the paper directly. These are all faithful, and it is worth
saying so explicitly before the list of deviations:

5.1  Peak/valley definition. Section V.A defines a peak as a grey level whose
     frequency is higher than BOTH immediate neighbours, and a valley as one
     lower than both. That is precisely argrelextrema(..., order=1). There is
     no prominence threshold, no minimum separation, no smoothing in the paper.
     Your detector implements the published rule.

     Importantly, the large peak count is NOT a bug and NOT noise-fitting: the
     paper's own worked example in Section V.A uses NPeaks = 52 to demonstrate
     Eq. (2). Your 55 is exactly the regime the authors expect.

5.2  Eq. (1) N-parameter = sum(Valley[i]) / NValleys. Correct.

5.3  Eq. (2) CBD = ceil(Ln(NPeaks)), same value for height and width. Correct.
     (Paper's example: NPeaks=52 -> ceil(3.951) = 4, so CBD 4x4.)

5.4  Eq. (3) I_CL = (Peaks[i] + N-parameter) / Max(Valley), evaluated PER PEAK
     inside the search loop. Correct - see 6.2 for the one clamp you added.

5.5  The multi-enhancement search. Section V.A: "the input image is subjected
     to the enhancement process multiple times. The frequency of enhancement
     process depends on the number of identified peaks ... this part tests each
     value of the identified peaks group to specify the peak that generates the
     optimum enhanced image." Your loop does exactly this, and it correctly
     scores each candidate on the WHOLE image, which is what the paper's own
     complexity analysis confirms: "(v) ... the CLAHE operation and PSNR,
     entropy and AMBE computation iterate over all image pixels for each peak,
     which results in a complexity of O(P.M.N)". Correct.

     (In my previous revision I flagged this as a possible mismatch between
     Part 1 and Part 2. The paper resolves it: whole-image scoring is intended.)

5.6  Eq. (4) CIQI = (PSNR + Entropy)/AMBE, selected by maximum. Correct, and
     the paper confirms the direction: "The proposed CLALHE requires high PSNR
     and entropy and the lowest AMBE value to obtain the highest or optimal
     CIQI." See 6.3 for what that implies.

5.7  Eq. (5) ONS, including the forced-even rule. Section V.B: "if the numbers
     of histogram valleys are 50 and 100 ... log2 produces 5.65 and 6.65. Thus
     the ONS values were set to 6 (ceil(5.65)=6) and 8 (ceil(6.65)+1=8)."
     Your code ceilings first, then adds 1 if odd - matching both examples.
     Correct. (My previous revision wrongly listed "force even" as your
     addition; it is in the paper.)

5.8  Eq. (6) SID. The printed equation is typographically mangled, but the
     paper's worked examples pin it down, on a 592-high x 896-wide image:
         ONS = 2  ->  Height = 592,    Width = 896/2 = 448
         ONS = 4  ->  Height = 592/2,  Width = 896/4 x 2 = 448
     i.e. for ONS>2, Height = M/2 and Width = N/(ONS/2), giving a 2 x (ONS/2)
     grid. Your `sub_h = h//2; sub_w = w//(ons//2)` is algebraically identical.
     Correct.

5.9  Global parameters applied to every subimage. Section V.B step (iv):
     "utilisation of the optimum enhancement parameters obtained previously in
     Part 1 for the enhancement of each subimage." The subimages do NOT
     re-derive their own CBD/I_CL. Your code is correct. (Previously flagged as
     unverifiable; now resolved.)

5.10 CLAHE (not plain HE) as the per-subimage operation. Confirmed by Section
     IX item (vii): "The application of CLAHE for each block". Correct.

5.11 Grid arithmetic. I verified across image sizes from 20x20 to 1280x959 and
     ONS values 2..10 that rows*cols always equals ONS, and that the remainder
     handling ("if r < rows-1 else h") leaves no gap or double-cover. Sound.

6. AUDIT - WHAT YOU MISSED OR ADDED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

6.1  MISSING: nothing about blending - because the paper specifies none.
     Covered in Section 3. Your original code was faithful; the artifact is
     the method's, not yours. The fix in Section 4 is a deliberate, documented
     departure from the published algorithm.

6.2  ADDED, AND IT CHANGES THE OUTPUT: the clamp `max(i_cl, 1.0)` in
     _calculate_i_cl. Eq. (3) has no such floor. On crybaby.jpeg:

         I_CL candidates from Eq. (3): min 0.371, max 1.210
         47 of 55 candidates fall below 1.0 and are pinned to 1.0 by the clamp

     The clamp makes your enhancement STRONGER than the paper's, not weaker.
     Compare the selected operating point:

                                 selected I_CL   PSNR    entropy   AMBE
         your code (clamped)         1.000       23.23    7.720   10.425
         Eq. (3) as published        0.371       30.95    7.564    4.759

     Now compare against what the paper reports for the Faces 1999 dataset
     (Table 8): PSNR 31.822, AMBE 3.464. The unclamped, paper-faithful run
     (30.95 / 4.76) lands right next to the published numbers; the clamped run
     (23.23 / 10.43) does not. That is good evidence the clamp is the single
     deviation pushing your results away from the paper's.

     Note OpenCV accepts clipLimit < 1 fine - it simply clips the histogram
     below the mean bin count, which is aggressive clipping and therefore a
     gentle enhancement. There is no numerical reason for the floor.

6.3  THE CIQI SEARCH NEVER ACTUALLY SEARCHES - and this is the paper's issue,
     not yours. CIQI = (PSNR + Entropy)/AMBE is strictly monotonically
     DECREASING in the clip limit. Measured on crybaby.jpeg at CBD=5:

         clip=0.05  PSNR 45.79  ent 7.457  AMBE  1.09  CIQI 48.82
         clip=0.20  PSNR 36.03  ent 7.513  AMBE  2.75  CIQI 15.85
         clip=0.37  PSNR 30.95  ent 7.564  AMBE  4.76  CIQI  8.09
         clip=1.00  PSNR 23.23  ent 7.720  AMBE 10.43  CIQI  2.97
         clip=2.00  PSNR 19.03  ent 7.867  AMBE 14.80  CIQI  1.82
         clip=5.00  PSNR 16.11  ent 7.963  AMBE 18.71  CIQI  1.29

     Entropy barely moves (7.46 -> 7.96 across a 100x change in clip limit)
     while AMBE grows by 17x and PSNR falls by 30 dB. So the ratio is dominated
     by AMBE, and argmax(CIQI) is provably equivalent to min(candidates).
     The "multiple enhancement" loop of Section V.A - the paper's headline
     novelty (i) - evaluates NPeaks candidates and then always returns the
     smallest one. It could be replaced by a one-line min() with identical
     output.

     Taken to its logical end, AMBE -> 0 sends CIQI -> infinity, so the global
     optimum of Eq. (4) is to not enhance the image at all. This is a genuine
     weakness of the published fitness function. It is also consistent with the
     paper's own results: CLALHE wins Table 8 on AMBE (lowest) and PSNR
     (highest) on every dataset, which is exactly what "changed the image
     least" produces. Worth raising if you are writing a critique.

6.4  ADDED: the 3-tap moving-average smoothing of the histogram in
     _find_peaks_valleys. Not in the paper - Section V.A compares raw
     frequencies. It is load-bearing:

                                   peaks  valleys  CBD  ONS   grid
         with your smoothing         55      54     5    6    2 x 3
         raw histogram (paper)       76      75     5    8    2 x 4

     So the smoothing changes the subdivision from 6 subimages to 8. If you
     want to match the paper, remove the convolution.

6.5  ADDED: other guards not present in the equations. Less consequential than
     6.2 and 6.4, but declare them if you write this up:
         - max(cbd, 2) in _calculate_cbd
         - the various fallbacks: n_peaks=1, n_valleys=4, CBD=4, I_CL=2.0,
           AMBE=0.001, max(ons,2), max(sub_h,1), max(sub_w,1)
     These only fire on degenerate histograms, so they do not affect your
     current results.

6.6  COLOUR HANDLING - a defensible deviation. Section V.A says the input "will
     be converted into a greyscale scheme ... because we only need to apply the
     technique to a single channel, which represents the image's intensity".
     The paper describes a greyscale pipeline, yet its result figures (12, 19,
     23) are in colour, so the text and figures do not fully agree and no
     colour-reconstruction step is ever specified. Your LAB L-channel route is
     a reasonable reading - LAB's L IS the intensity channel, and it lets you
     rebuild a colour output while leaving hue untouched. Just flag it as an
     interpretation, since the paper does not state it.

6.7  MINOR: PSNR constant. Eq. (10) is PSNR = 10 log10((Max(Ii))^2 / MSE),
     where Max(Ii) is "the maximum grey level intensity to the original image".
     You use 255 unconditionally. That is the conventional choice and it only
     matters for images that never reach 255, but it is not literally Eq. (10).

6.8  MINOR: _calculate_i_cl internally does np.max(peak_values) with the
     comment "use the maximum peak", which contradicts the per-peak intent of
     Eq. (3). It is harmless because part1 calls it with a one-element array
     inside the loop, so the correct per-peak behaviour is what runs. Still
     worth tidying so the function states one intent.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You did not miss a blending or interpolation step, because the paper does not
have one. "Bilinear interpolation" appears once in the whole paper and it is
describing a different method (ACE-HM, ref [78]) in the literature review.
For CLALHE itself, Section IX explicitly calls the merge a "concatenation".
Your original part2_enhance implemented the published Part 2 correctly, and the
seams follow directly from it: each subimage is equalized through its own
lookup table and the results are butted together with hard edges, so brightness
jumps across every boundary. The paper's evaluation could not detect this -
PSNR, entropy, AMBE, SSI, CII and RMSE are all global statistics that a few
boundary rows barely move.

The fix keeps every element of the paper's algorithm - ONS, SID, subimage count
and placement, independent per-subimage histograms, the Part 1 parameters - and
changes only the compositing: each subimage is read with a 25% context margin
and merged through a linear cross-fade whose weights sum to 1 at every
boundary. Measured seam strength drops to the level of the original photograph.

Separately, two things in your code do differ from the paper and both are worth
fixing or at least declaring: the `max(i_cl, 1.0)` clamp, which moves the
selected clip limit from 0.371 to 1.0 and pushes your PSNR/AMBE away from the
paper's published numbers (6.2); and the histogram smoothing, which changes ONS
from 8 to 6 and therefore the whole subdivision (6.4).

And one finding that belongs to the paper rather than to you: maximising CIQI
over the peak candidates is provably equivalent to picking the smallest clip
limit, so the "multiple enhancement" search - contribution (i) of Section VI -
never explores anything (6.3).
